Intent (2026-11-18)

The only thing that matters now is how you steer agents to achieve an objective. Nobody can afford to be doing work about work or meetings about meetings when AI can do most grunt work. Cheap creation has a cost: The Pile, the output someone must judge. Steering starts with a clear intent.

As AI spreads through every aspect of companies, a lot of people are now talking about taste as the final thing that remains a human competitive advantage. Unfortunately, taste is a somewhat vague term that can be both dismissed as too basic to be a moat but also as something extremely complex to develop.  This is especially true among more technical readers who may associate taste more with pure artforms like music.

At its core, taste is really a synonym for judgment. The question is now, do you think you can instil good judgment in an individual, in a team, in a whole company? The answer in most cases is obviously yes. The corporate world has successfully created millions of people that can exercise good judgment, but this has usually come from many years of experience. Good judgment doesn’t mean that every decision is correct, but it does mean picking the most reasonable option under limited information. The basics of good judgment start with having clear intent to measure those options against. When limited information is available, the one thing you always have available is “what am I trying to achieve by doing this?”

What years of experience buys you is really two things: a feel for what good looks like and a record of the consequences of all the previous decisions you made.

For the first, a basic description of your intent written down isn’t enough to decide what is good. It gets sharpened by the choices made against it that reveal what you were optimising for. The memory of consequences came from years of grunt work which, unfortunately for the future of corporations, is the learning mechanism that AI is pulling from underneath us. So how do we train future decision makers in good judgment now?

The answer is of course giving people real decisions much earlier, on a slice of work that they own, while agents execute. We call this Owner mode, where you convert juniors from being pure executors to decision makers. Since each person gets to make decisions and see what happens, they rapidly build a record of consequences which sharpens their understanding of what good looks like. To truly make this work you need good mentorship where someone more senior reviews the choices against their more refined understanding of intent.

There are normally two reasons delegating more responsibility to juniors doesn’t work. Firstly, juniors lack information to decide and secondly, they’re afraid of getting it wrong so won’t pitch upwards. On the information side, agents can easily handle large amounts of context and be primed with the company’s intent which closes the gap that someone being dropped into a new company would typically experience. On getting it wrong, agents let you rapidly and cheaply iterate on multiple options where a junior may otherwise have only executed on one thing. Choosing from multiple options is, and feels like, making a judgment call whereas executing on one thing manually requires you to make a bigger bet you then feel the need to defend. If nothing consequential can happen without approval (both due to access controls and agents needing explicit approval from the user to take certain actions), then experimentation becomes psychologically safe. 

A junior on the sales team writing outbound copy can be empowered with an agent that has access to the founders’ vision, the company’s mission, all the content the marketing team has tried before. What they do is come up with multiple ideas and have their agent create them all then make a choice. Their mentor then refines their understanding by asking a simple question: “why that one?” The junior says it opens with the customer’s problem instead of the product. The mentor agrees, then adds what only experience knows: this company never leads with a discount, because its buyers distrust them. That one exchange sharpens the junior’s sense of what good looks like, and the next round starts from a sharper intent.

This is also how you most effectively work with frontier AI agents, iteratively refining intent until you get the result you want. When you think about it this is what all levels of (competent) management do, all the way up to the founders.

People who read Paul Graham’s essays may have noticed that Owner mode sounds similar to Founder mode [1]. That’s deliberate. In the essay, founders stay close to the details of the company instead of treating each team as a black box. My theory is that this is just a way to carry the founder’s intent through the whole organization and a founder is best positioned to do this by virtue of being most obsessed about the customer’s problem and the quality of the product. It is true that founders have authority and soft power that juniors don’t, but the principle travels: organizations are strongest when everyone is pulling in the same direction, and that takes more than telling people what to do. People need to be in control of their own piece of the work, and to understand not just the what of what they’re doing but the why in the context of the mission.

Owner mode converts juniors from executors into decision makers, and it flattens the org chart, which makes Founder mode easier to implement. Information is no longer gated by layers of managers, because every decision and the reasons behind it are on record. A founder can read why a choice was made, or ask the person who made it, without waiting for a status update or a meeting. The same agents that speed up the work also make it visible, and no company has ever had that view at this scale. The box stays open. And the question they ask is the one the mentor asks the junior: why that one?

This model only works if the below are all true:

1.	Cull processes that shouldn’t exist and have your agent automate anything cheap and reversible. 
2.	Proactive agents eliminate the coordination layer and keep each team member on top of their own work. Many current agent systems get this wrong: they are too loud and too frequent, so people ignore them or switch them off. Loud agents just add to The Pile instead of reducing it. Creating more of everything is trivial now. Designing agents that create less is hard. An agent should step in only when something important is about to be dropped, or when it can propose a system that takes grunt work off your plate. People generally aren’t systems thinkers, so their agents need to have a framework for when to systemize.
3.	Agents read broadly and act narrowly. Perhaps the most controversial claim in this essay is that data siloing is disastrous for agent-driven companies, so do as little of it as possible and only for regulatory or security reasons. In practice, the engineering team’s agents should be able to read the sales team’s collateral, and customer support’s agents should be able to read the codebase. What agents can do should remain gated by access controls and explicit approval. To quote John Gilmore, “The Net interprets censorship as damage, and routes around it.” [2] Teams treat blocked access the same way and the highest performing teams will be heavily incentivized to build shadow IT to become more effective if you don’t provide approved routes for this.
4.	The loop from intent to outcome must be as tight as possible. Intent → Artifact → Decision → Outcome. This is the most important requirement of all. It must be trivial to parallelize multiple pieces of work. The artifact, be that a PR or marketing copy must be front and center and the ability to interrogate the agent for how it made its decisions and how that matches your intent must be simple and fast. Practically this means voice input and text output with minimal UI to maximize readable space and prevent you bike-shedding [3]. Of course, running many things in parallel or multiple experiments for the same thing risks adding to The Pile so it’s crucial to have the agent bin bad experiments and make at-a-glance review trivial.

AI will keep making more and The Pile will grow. The companies that win won’t be the ones that make the most volume, but the ones where the most people can run the tightest judgment loops. The way to make this happen is to enable Owner mode for everyone: a slice of work to own, real decisions to make, and a record of what happened. Taste isn’t mystical. Intent, applied in a tight loop, becomes taste.
